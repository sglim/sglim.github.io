We are hiring. Which means I am interviewing. Which means this month I have spent more hours listening to strangers solve problems on a whiteboard than writing code myself.

I have been on the other side of this a lot. The one that stuck with me was the internship interview at the search company, years ago. I remember the interviewer not saying anything for what felt like five minutes while I wrote a loop. Later, when I got in, I found out the silence was on purpose. They wanted to see what you do when nobody helps you. I hated it at the time. I use it now.

Some things I have figured out after maybe twenty interviews.

The question matters less than I thought. I spent a week designing the perfect question. Then I watched a colleague ask "reverse a linked list" and learn more about the candidate in thirty minutes than I did with my clever graph problem. What you learn comes from the follow ups. Why did you pick that structure. What happens if the input is huge. What would you change if you had another hour.

Nervous is not the same as weak. Some people freeze for the first ten minutes and then become excellent. If I judge the first ten minutes I lose those people. So I started with a warm up that does not count. Small talk about their last project. By the time we get to the real problem they have remembered they know how to talk.

Watch what they do when they are wrong. Everyone is wrong at some point in an hour. The ones I want to work with say "wait, that's not right" and fix it. The ones I worry about defend the wrong answer or wait for me to tell them. That one signal predicts more than the final code.

Write down what you saw, not what you felt. My first few feedback notes said things like "strong" and "not sure". Useless a week later. Now I write "took twelve minutes to get a working solution, missed the empty input case, found it when I asked about edge cases, explanation was clear". Somebody else can read that and disagree with my conclusion, which is the point.

The part I did not expect is how tiring it is. An hour of interviewing costs me two hours of real work. Not because of the hour itself but because I keep thinking about it after. Did I ask that fairly. Did I let my mood from the last meeting leak in. There is a person on the other side of the table whose next year depends partly on whether I paid attention.

We are small enough that every hire changes the team. Number eight will change how number five works. I take that seriously, maybe too seriously. But I would rather be the interviewer who overthinks it than the one who checks the box.

Back to the whiteboard tomorrow. Two more.
