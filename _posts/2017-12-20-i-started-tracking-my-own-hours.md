Since the start of this year I have been writing down what I work on. Not for the company. For me. A spreadsheet, one row per block of time, a category, a note. Meetings count. Commuting does not. Lunch does not.

I want to explain why, and what a year of it showed me.

The why goes back a while. My first real program that other people used was in the air force. I was a computer specialist, which mostly meant fixing printers, but at some point I built an emergency contact system for the whole branch. Who to call, in what order, when something happens at 3am. It was not sophisticated. It was used. I remember a general shaking my hand about it and someone taking a photo, and I remember thinking: I have no idea how many hours that took me. I could not have told you if it was forty or four hundred. That bothered me for years.

So this January I started counting.

What a year looks like, from January 12th when I started to today:

- A little over two thousand hours logged, across about 270 days.
- Around 85 percent of it went to the travel app. Development, meetings, working from home, all of it. That is correct. It is my main job and this was the year we launched.
- Meetings, across everything, came to about one hour in eight. I thought it was more. It feels like more. Feelings are not data.
- Interviews were six hours. Six. We are growing and I have a feeling next year that number will look very different.
- Company stuff, the all hands, the hackathon week, the website redo, was under five percent. Smaller than I expected. I suspect this grows as the company does.
- Weekends barely show up. A handful of hours across the whole year. Good.

Things the data changed:

I stopped saying "I have no time". I have time. I have a lot of time. Some of it goes to things I did not choose and some goes to things I chose badly, but the total is not small. Saying "I have no time" was a way of not looking at the sheet.

I noticed which categories make me tired versus which take hours. They are not the same. Two hours of interviewing costs me more than four hours of server work. The sheet does not show energy but once I saw the hours I could feel the gap.

I got better at estimating. When someone asks how long a feature takes, I now have a year of "I thought two days, it took four" to calibrate against. My multiplier is about 1.7. Yours is probably similar. Write it down and find out.

Things the data did not change:

I still work too many hours some weeks. Knowing is not the same as stopping. But at least now when I do it, I do it on purpose, and I can see it on the sheet the next Monday, and I feel a little embarrassed, and that is worth something.

I'm going to keep going. If I'm still doing this in three years I'll have a real picture of what this job actually is, which is not what the title says.
