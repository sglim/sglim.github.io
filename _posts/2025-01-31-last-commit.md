My last commit at the startup went in yesterday. A fix to an image cleanup job. Small, correct, unglamorous. That is how I would want it.

I am leaving. We are going back to Korea.

I am not going to write about why in detail. Some of it is the pull toward home I wrote about in November, which got stronger rather than weaker. Some of it is the job, which was good in the ways I said and less good in ways I will not put on a public blog. Not every ending is a clean one. This one was not. I have made peace with that and I do not need to relitigate it here.

What I want to write about instead is what ten months of that job left me with, because I will forget the good parts if I do not.

I learned to ship at a speed I had not worked at before. Tuesday change, Wednesday production, no ceremony. It is terrifying for the first month and then it is just how things are, and you realize that most of the ceremony at bigger companies exists to make people feel safe rather than to make software safe.

I learned that smoke tests and backfills are the same at every company, and that I am apparently the person who shows up and builds them. I have stopped resisting that. Someone has to be the person who cares whether the data is right, and being that person is a fine thing to be.

I learned to work in one language, one time zone, one room. After the two hump years it was a relief and I did not fully appreciate it until now, when I am about to move back across the ocean and become the far side again for whoever I work with next.

I relearned, the hard way, that a small company is its people, and that when the people are right it is the best job in the world and when they are not there is nothing else to fall back on. I have now seen both within the same decade. I know what to look for next time.

A small note on data. My time tracker stopped in May. Seven years and four months, from January 2017 to May 2024, across two apps and five jobs. I do not have a good reason for stopping. The company laptop, a new routine, a habit that quietly ended. I noticed in the summer and did not restart it. I might again. Or I might decide that seven years of knowing I work two thousand hours a year is enough, and the eighth year would not tell me anything new.

New York, two years and seven months. I came for English and a different way of working and I got both. I am leaving with a better resume, a worse back from a bad chair, a small number of real friends, and an accent I no longer apologize for.

Flights are booked for February. There will be boxes again. Then a few months of figuring out what Korea looks like now, and then whatever is next.

The image cleanup job is running. It will keep running after I am gone. That is the most a last commit can ask for.
